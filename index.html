Energy Cost Shocks & the Global System

Case Study: Strait of Hormuz Crisis 2026 · UNMdP

1. Introduction & Motivation

The Strait of Hormuz constitutes one of the most critical energy chokepoints in the global economy. In early 2026, geopolitical escalation led to a sustained disruption of transit flows equivalent to approximately 20% of global oil supply and a significant share of seaborne LNG trade.

This research examines how such a sharp, geopolitically driven energy cost shock propagates through the macroeconomic system and financial markets, with particular attention to differential regional impacts — especially the high energy-import dependence of Japan and other Asian economies.

4. Macroeconomic Transmission Channels

An energy cost shock operates primarily as a negative supply shock. It raises production costs, erodes real household income, and can trigger second-round effects via inflation expectations and wage-setting.

Direct channels

  • Higher input costs for energy-intensive sectors
  • Pass-through to consumer energy prices
  • Terms-of-trade deterioration for net importers

Indirect / financial channels

  • Tighter financial conditions via inflation risk premia
  • Equity market volatility and sectoral reallocation
  • Potential monetary policy response (FED, ECB, BOJ)

Rule-of-thumb (IMF / Goldman Sachs): a sustained 10% rise in oil prices typically reduces global GDP growth by ≈0.1–0.15 pp and raises inflation by ≈0.3–0.4 pp.

6. Implications for Financial Markets

Energy cost shocks affect asset pricing through higher discount rates, cash-flow uncertainty and shifts in risk premia. Volatility itself becomes a priced factor.

Equity market volatility

GARCH / EGARCH models quantify the rise in conditional variance and leverage effects during the shock window.

Credit spreads & risk premia

Corporate bond spreads typically widen as growth outlooks deteriorate and uncertainty rises.

Cross-asset spillovers

Energy futures, equities and safe-haven assets exhibit time-varying correlations that can be examined with multivariate GARCH.

7. Regional Asymmetries — Focus on Japan

Japan presents a particularly informative case: extreme dependence on Middle Eastern crude (90–96% of imports) combined with one of the deepest strategic petroleum reserve systems among IEA members.

Vulnerability

  • Very high Middle East crude dependence
  • Limited domestic production
  • Exposure to Asian LNG price spikes

Resilience factors

  • Strategic reserves ≈ 250 days of consumption
  • Early and large stock releases in 2026
  • Rapid diversification of supply sources

Key references: Atlantic Council (2026), Daiwa Institute, Oxford Energy Forum, METI/JOGMEC.

8. Conclusions & Research Outlook

The 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption provides a rare laboratory for studying the real and financial transmission of a large, geopolitically driven energy supply shock. Evidence points to significant but heterogeneous effects, with Asia bearing a disproportionate share of the adjustment.

By combining a transparent macro narrative with rigorous time-series methods (SVAR + GARCH), this work delivers pedagogical clarity for the course and a solid empirical foundation for further master’s-level research.
