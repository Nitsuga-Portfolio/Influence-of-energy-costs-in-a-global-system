Energy Cost Shocks & the Global System

Case Study: Strait of Hormuz Crisis 2026 · UNMdP

1. Introduction & Motivation

The Strait of Hormuz constitutes one of the most critical energy chokepoints in the global economy. In early 2026, geopolitical escalation led to a sustained disruption of transit flows equivalent to approximately 20% of global oil supply and a significant share of seaborne LNG trade.

This research examines how such a sharp, geopolitically driven energy cost shock propagates through the macroeconomic system and financial markets, with particular attention to differential regional impacts — especially the high energy-import dependence of Japan and other Asian economies.

Research Question: How do sharp energy cost shocks transmit into inflation, growth, equity and credit-market volatility, and what explains the heterogeneous resilience observed across major economies — particularly Japan?

2. The 2026 Hormuz Shock — Stylised Facts

The disruption ranks among the largest supply shocks in the history of the modern oil market. Benchmark prices reacted sharply, while physical shortages and elevated freight/insurance costs created significant regional price differentials, particularly affecting Asia and Europe.

3. Macroeconomic Transmission Channels

An energy cost shock operates primarily as a negative supply shock. It simultaneously raises production costs, erodes real household income, and can trigger second-round effects via inflation expectations and wage-setting.

Direct channels

  • Higher input costs for energy-intensive sectors
  • Pass-through to consumer energy prices
  • Terms-of-trade deterioration for net importers

Indirect / financial channels

  • Tighter financial conditions via higher inflation risk premia
  • Equity market volatility and sectoral reallocation
  • Potential monetary policy response (FED, ECB, BOJ)

Rule-of-thumb estimates (IMF / Goldman Sachs): a sustained 10% increase in oil prices typically reduces global GDP growth by ≈0.1–0.15 pp and raises inflation by ≈0.3–0.4 pp, with larger effects for highly import-dependent economies.

5. Implications for Financial Markets

Beyond the real economy, energy cost shocks affect asset pricing through multiple channels: higher discount rates (via inflation risk), increased cash-flow uncertainty for energy-intensive firms, and shifts in risk premia. Volatility itself becomes a priced factor.

Equity market volatility

GARCH and EGARCH models allow us to quantify the increase in conditional variance and test for leverage effects during the disruption window.

Credit spreads & risk premia

Corporate bond spreads (especially for energy-intensive and emerging-market issuers) typically widen as growth outlooks deteriorate and uncertainty rises.

Cross-asset spillovers

Energy futures, equities, and safe-haven assets (yen, gold) exhibit time-varying correlations that can be examined via multivariate GARCH or dynamic conditional correlation models.

6. Regional Asymmetries — Focus on Japan

Japan presents a particularly informative case: extreme dependence on Middle Eastern crude (approximately 90–96% of imports) combined with one of the deepest strategic petroleum reserve systems among IEA members and a highly developed policy response capacity.

Vulnerability

  • Very high Middle East crude dependence
  • Limited domestic production
  • Exposure to Asian LNG price spikes

Resilience factors

  • Strategic reserves ≈ 250 days of consumption
  • Early and large stock releases in 2026
  • Rapid diversification of supply sources

Key references: Atlantic Council (2026), Daiwa Institute of Research, Oxford Energy Forum, METI/JOGMEC analyses.

7. Conclusions & Research Outlook

The 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption provides a rare high-frequency laboratory for studying the real and financial transmission of a large, geopolitically driven energy supply shock. Preliminary evidence points to significant but heterogeneous effects across regions, with Asia bearing a disproportionate share of the adjustment burden.

By combining a transparent macro narrative with rigorous time-series methods (SVAR + GARCH), this work aims to deliver both pedagogical clarity for the undergraduate course and a solid empirical foundation suitable for further development at the master’s research level.

Identification Strategy

The core challenge is to isolate the exogenous component of the energy price movement attributable to the Hormuz disruption from endogenous demand-driven fluctuations.

  • 1. External instrument / proxy SVAR: Use high-frequency price changes around key geopolitical announcements or physical flow disruptions as an instrument for the energy-price shock (following the literature on oil-market identification).
  • 2. Narrative / event-study approach: Define a clear event window around the onset of the sustained disruption (early March 2026) and estimate local projections.
  • 3. Robustness: Compare results under alternative lag lengths, variable sets, and sample periods (pre- vs. post-shock).

Econometric Models

1. Structural VAR (core specification)

A small-scale SVAR in the spirit of the oil-market literature (Kilian, Baumeister & Hamilton). Typical endogenous vector: [energy price, inflation, industrial production / activity, equity return, credit spread].

2. GARCH-family models (volatility layer)

Univariate GARCH(1,1), EGARCH and GJR-GARCH estimated on daily equity and energy-futures returns to quantify the increase in conditional volatility and asymmetric responses during the shock window.

3. Local Projections (robustness / complementary)

Jordà local projections around the event date provide a flexible, non-parametric check on the impulse responses obtained from the SVAR.
